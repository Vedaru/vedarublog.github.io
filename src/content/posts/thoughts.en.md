# So I gave up on medicine  

When I was still in primary school I had an operation, the general anaesthetic kind. A pure white operating theatre and the acrid smell of disinfectant: together they formed my first idea of hospitals and of medicine.  

When the college entrance exam was over and I had to choose a university and a major, I picked a medical school. There was no lofty motive; it simply got me into the university I wanted. I was not actually interested in the core courses in the programme, though a few of the general electives were genuinely interesting.

But as this term's courses drew to a close, I finally decided to give up medicine.

Let me be clear: this was not because medicine is hard. On the contrary, the deeper I went, the more I came to see medicine as an extraordinarily noble undertaking. It is not cold data and dissection; it is humanity trying to use finite reason against infinite uncertainty, to guard the dignity of life's last line of defence.

I left because, from the window of that ivory tower, I saw an unfathomable crack outside it.

I felt a confusion I had never felt before. I do not understand why, when a plane crashes, people treat it as a systemic tragedy, a sacrifice to probability, and seek technological reform in their mourning; while when a serious medical accident happens, public opinion turns immediately and points at a specific individual.

That helplessness has a bloody footnote. I still cannot forget what happened to Dr Tao Yong. When the story of the farmer and the snake played out in reality in such a brutal way, what I heard was the sound of a professional faith shattering. And in the discourse that followed, besides brief sympathy, there was the chilling malice of "flies don't land on a crackless egg". I also know that bad actors like Liu Xiangfeng of Xiangya exist, but what frightens me is that the public has grown used to generalising anger at an individual into a presumption of guilt against an entire profession.

**I tried to examine this absurdity from a more philosophical angle, and found that it may be a pathology of modernity itself.**

We live in a disenchanted age. With religion withdrawn, **medicine has been forced to take over territory that once belonged to theology.** The hospital became the church of modern people, and doctors were made to wear the priest's robes. People subconsciously believe that so long as technology advances enough, death will cease to be an inevitable destination and become a "technical fault" that can be postponed indefinitely.

So when treatment fails, the patient feels not only physical pain but the betrayal of a promise. They cannot accept the inevitability of what Heidegger called being-towards-death, and they concretise their metaphysical fear of death into real hatred of the doctor. Because hating a concrete person (the doctor) is far easier than hating an intangible fate (death). The doctor becomes the innocent scapegoat humanity needs in the face of its own mortality.

Moreover, **Cartesian mind-body dualism** seems to have reached a morbid extreme today. People treat their bodies as a car, a watch, an **object** separate from the self.
They walk into the hospital with the logic of repairing a machine: "I paid the repair fee, so you must make the machine run again." Yet medicine deals with biological systems full of chaos and contingency, not with a tightly fitted mechanical structure. This endless expansion of **instrumental reason** alienates the human care in medical treatment into a bargaining chip.

When Western medicine honestly states its uncertainty, it is called incompetent or evasive; when certain unproven traditional therapies offer vague comfort, they are elevated to gospel because they satisfy people's psychological omnipotence. This is not only a lack of scientific understanding but a kind of stress-induced regression in the public psyche before industrialised medicine.

I saw all of this, but I did not have the courage to be the Sisyphus trying to fill the crack.

I still admire those who stay at the operating table. They are the real heroes, warriors who keep suturing wounds under fire, Camus-style rebels who face the void and still choose responsibility.

But I chose to run. I cannot, in a context that treats "buying health" as a given and "death" as medical malpractice, carry out a vocation that ought to rest on absolute trust.

Medicine is sacred, but it cannot bear humanity's excessive greed for immortality and certainty. And I just want to live a little more simply.

# Why don't young people have children now? A philosophical diagnosis of existence, nihilism and courage

When we talk about "young people not having children", what do we usually talk about?

Soaring housing prices, a hyper-competitive workplace, expensive education, or the hard balance women strike between career and family. All of that is true. But I have always felt that these economic and social explanations touch the skin without reaching the bone.

If we swap the microscope for a telescope and examine the phenomenon from a **philosophical** angle, you find it is not merely a choice about a wallet but a collective existential crisis about who I am, where time went, and whether life means anything.

## The curse of reason: when a child becomes a liability

The defining feature of modern society is what Max Weber called disenchantment and the rule of instrumental reason.

In the past, having children belonged to **value rationality**. It was a gift from heaven, the order of nature, an unquestionable sacred duty. You did not calculate how much a child would cost, just as you did not calculate the cost of breathing.

But now **everything has been folded into cost-benefit analysis.**

When young people examine childbearing with the "reason" modern society taught them, they find it a thoroughly **irrational** act:
> You must invest enormous freedom, money and energy (high cost), face great uncertainty (high risk), and the return is often intangible, delayed, or absent entirely (low return).

In a society that demands maximum efficiency, not having children is the most logical choice for a "rational actor".

## The burden of the self and the exhaustion of desire

Beyond the arithmetic, young people today carry an **overload of subjectivity**.

Sartre tells us existence precedes essence; Foucault hints that we should sculpt the self like a work of art. So each of us becomes an unfinished **project**. We are busy exercising, collecting certificates, getting promoted, going to therapy, busy **becoming a better self**.

What does having a child mean? It means this grand "self project" is forced to stop, and that you must shift your focus from "me" to "him". For modern people used to being self-centred, that is an ontological conflict.

Worse, as Byung-Chul Han argues in The Burnout Society, excessive self-exploitation has already left us **drained of vitality**.

This tiredness does not go away with sleep. It is a tiredness of the soul. Many young people no longer have the strength to love themselves; where would the surplus energy (Eros) come from to create and nourish a new life?

> **They are not selfish. They are struggling on the red line of survival, with nothing left over for the adventure of life.**

## The conflict with the older generation: genes versus memes

This explains why young people and their elders cannot communicate about "carrying on the line".

The older philosophy is a **philosophy of the chain**. Life is a long river; the individual is a bridge between ancestors and descendants. Not having children breaks the chain, betrays the ancestors, means a void after death.

But the younger philosophy has changed.
1.  **From genes to memes**: young people think that leaving thoughts, code or videos on the internet (memes) is a more real trace than leaving bloodline (genes).
2.  **Refusing to pass on suffering**: what the elders call "cultural transmission" often looks to young people like stale discipline and trauma.
    *   *"If transmitting means making my child suffer everything I suffered — the rat race, the mortgage, the discipline — then not transmitting is the greater mercy."*
3.  **Facing the void**: modern philosophy has let young people see through the illusion of "symbolic immortality". Since humanity will eventually perish, why pretend to live forever by having children?

**Not having a child becomes a morally charged blocking mechanism, a refusal of a bad script.**

## The hard question: so should you have children or not?

Having listed all the reasons to back off, should young people really **not** have children?

By pure rational calculation, the answer is indeed "no". **But philosophy does not only deconstruct; it also looks for meaning.**

If we step outside utilitarianism and look from an **existentialist** angle, having children still has irreplaceable value. Here are a few coordinates for judgement:

### 1. A Kantian test
Ask yourself: do you want a child in order to have someone to care for you in old age (a means), or simply to welcome a life (an end)?
> **"Treat humanity as an end, never merely as a means."**
If you can avoid treating the child as a tool and expect nothing in return, purely as giving, then having a child takes on a moral nobility.

### 2. Escaping the prison of the self
Are you tired of that narrow, anxious "self"?
Levinas argued that responsibility for the other lets us transcend the self. Raising a child is a compulsory **decentring** experience. It is hard, but it breaks the lonely walls inside you and rebuilds a deep, flesh-and-blood connection with the world.

### 3. Kierkegaard's leap of faith
The return on having a child is guaranteed to be negative. You can never derive the decision to have one from reason.
Having a child is essentially what Kierkegaard called the **leap of faith**.

It is an **irrational heroism**: after seeing the absurdity, pain and meaninglessness of life, still choosing to create life and bet on the future.

## Closing

So when we talk about not having children, we are really talking about the predicament of modernity.

**Not having children is the victory of reason, the preservation of the self.**
**Having children is the courage of existence, a bet on the future.**

Neither is superior to the other.

In this uncertain age, may your choice — whether to end the line or continue it — come from your **genuinely free will**, and not from fear or conformity.
